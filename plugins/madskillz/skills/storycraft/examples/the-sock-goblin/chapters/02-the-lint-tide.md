# Chapter 2 — The Lint Tide

Mar lifted the colander one inch.

"Start talking," she said.

Pib straightened his dryer-sheet jacket and held the striped sock a little tighter. "If you lift that all the way," he said, "I'll show you faster than I can explain it."

Mar thought about this. Pib's amber eyes were steady. They did not look like liar eyes. She had written descriptions of liar eyes in her notebook.

She lifted the colander all the way.

Pib didn't run. He turned toward the dark gap behind the dryer and waved for her to follow.

Mar followed.

---

The gap was barely wide enough for her hand. Behind the dryer drum, the heat had dried the dust into something strange — a slow, soft current of warm air that smelled like lavender and old cotton. Bits of lint drifted through it like snow falling sideways.

"The Lint Tide," Pib said. He kept his voice low, the way you do near something powerful. "It blows every time the dryer runs. It goes under the drum, through the gap, and into my burrow." He pointed to a small round hole in the baseboard, neatly edged with a ring of buttons. "But here — right here — is the real trouble."

He aimed his finger at a spot near the bottom of the drum gap. A dark crescent-shaped hole, barely wider than two fingers.

"Socks catch on the lip of the drum," Pib said. "One at a time. Always the odd one — the one nearest the gap. The Lint Tide pulls it in, and then they fall through *that.*" He pointed to the crescent. "Without me, they're gone. Lost in the walls. Forever."

Mar crouched and peered at the crescent hole. She could see a sock in there right now — white with blue stripes, wedged in the dark.

Her sock.

She sat back on her heels and looked at Pib. "You've been fishing them out," she said.

"Rescuing," Pib said firmly. "The word is rescuing."

"With what?"

"I had a very good hook made from a paperclip and some thread." He patted his jacket pocket sadly. "I dropped it on Tuesday. That's why I hadn't gotten yours back yet."

Mar opened her notebook. She chewed her pen cap. Then she tore out a page.

"I have string," she said. "Do you have anything we could use for a net?"

Pib's whole face changed — ears lifting, eyes going bright. He disappeared into his burrow and came back with a piece of mesh from an old laundry bag, smaller than a playing card, with the edges sewn tight.

"I've been planning this for a while," he said.

"I can tell," said Mar.

They worked together on the laundry room floor. Mar cut the string in lengths and Pib tied the knots — his small hands were faster than hers. He made knots Mar had never seen before, knots that looked like tiny flowers. In seven minutes they had a net attached to a loop of string.

Mar lowered it into the crescent hole. She could feel the Lint Tide breathing warm air across her wrist.

She swept the net sideways.

Something thumped against the mesh.

She drew it up slowly.

The striped sock came with it, dusty but whole.

"Oh," said Mar softly.

Pib let out a long, quiet breath and pressed one small hand to his chest.

Mar shook the sock out and held it up. Both stripes were still there. She folded it neatly enough and set it on top of the dryer.

Then she opened her notebook to the page that said *SOCK GOBLIN — SUSPECT.*

She drew one line through the word *SUSPECT.*

She wrote two words underneath.

*Ally. (Pib.)*

She showed it to him.

Pib's ears rose slowly, like a question turning into an answer.

"Technically," he said, "this is the best notebook I have ever been written into."

Mar capped her pen. "It's the only notebook you've ever been written into."

"Yes," said Pib. "That's what makes it the best."

Mar put the notebook in her pocket. The Lint Tide breathed softly through the gap. On top of the dryer, the striped sock waited to be reunited with its pair.
